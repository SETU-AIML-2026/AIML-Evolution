# Explanation, and Where It Stops

Booklet Chapter 12. `explain.py` gives you three ways to explain one decision
and one way to summarise the model overall.

```python
import explain
local = explain.lime_from_scratch(model, train[FEATURES], x)   # no dependencies
shap_v = explain.shap_values(model, train[FEATURES], X)        # needs shap
lib    = explain.lime_library(model, train[FEATURES], x)       # needs lime
gi     = explain.global_importance(model, test[FEATURES], test.repaid)
```

`lime_from_scratch` is thirty lines: perturb the input, watch the output move,
fit a sparse linear model to the neighbourhood. It exists so the lab never
blocks on a failed `shap` or `lime` install, and so you can read a local
explainer end to end in one sitting.

## The trap sitting in those two tables

`postcode_score` is fourth globally and *last* locally. A stakeholder reads that
and concludes the proxy is not really being used.

But page 2 showed `postcode_score` recovering group membership almost perfectly,
and page 7 will show that deleting it makes every disparity worse. All three
facts are true at once.

The resolution: an attribution method reports how much a feature moved **this
prediction**, given the others. When several features carry the same information
— and `historical_bias` made sure income, savings and credit history all carry
some of it — no single one of them looks decisive. The information is
distributed, so the attribution is too.

> This is the most common way a SHAP plot is misread in industry. Low attribution
> to a proxy is not evidence the proxy is unused. It is frequently evidence that
> the proxy has company.

## The four limits, on your own output

1. **An explanation describes the model, not the decision's justification.** Your
   table says income drove the refusal. It has no opinion on whether income
   *should* drive a refusal, or on the fact that income here records access
   rather than ability. The moral work is entirely yours, and producing the
   attribution can feel like discharging it.
2. **Post-hoc explanations can be unfaithful.** They are local approximations to
   a non-linear model, and they look equally confident when they are wrong.
   Check faithfulness: perturb the features the explanation named and see whether
   the prediction moves as predicted.
3. **Methods disagree.** Run all three on the same applicant and compare the rank
   correlation. That is Exercise 3. If your process is "produce an explanation",
   disagreement is invisible. If your process is "produce two and compare", it is
   a finding.
4. **They can be gamed.** Published attacks build models that behave one way on
   real inputs and another on the perturbed inputs an explainer generates.
   Explanation is not an audit.

## What explanation is genuinely good for

Do not read the caveats as a case against the tools. Local explanation is the
single best practical technique for catching a proxy: when a refusal is
dominated by postcode, an attribution method shows it in seconds on a laptop,
and that is how a great many real fairness problems are actually found.

It also supports debugging, model comparison, stakeholder conversation, and the
"meaningful information about the logic involved" that GDPR Article 15(1)(h)
requires — which the Court in *Dun & Bradstreet Austria* read as the procedure
and principles actually applied, stated intelligibly, rather than a formula dump.

If you cannot write a paragraph a refused applicant could read, naming which
factors moved their score and in which direction, you do not yet have a
deployable system.
