## How to read it

Read along each row, not down the columns. The bolded cell is the gap that was
tuned. The other two cells are what it cost.

Row one buys demographic parity for a ten-point false-positive gap and a
sixteen-point predictive-parity gap. Row three is the ugliest: forcing the score
to mean the same thing after thresholding requires a group-B cut of 0.80 against
group A's 0.44, and blows the selection gap out to 56 points.

**No row zeroes all three.** Not because the search grid was too coarse, and not
because the optimiser gave up. Because no such configuration exists.

## Why not

The two escape routes are equal base rates and a perfect classifier.

Check both on your own run. Base rates: 0.548 and 0.437 — not equal. Accuracy:
0.699 — not perfect. Neither escape is available, so the trade-off binds, and it
would bind on any model you fitted to this data.

The intuition from Chapter 9, worth carrying without the algebra: calibration
ties the score to the observed frequency of the outcome within each group. If
the outcome is more frequent in one group, a calibrated score must average
higher there. A higher average against a fixed threshold means more positive
predictions, and among people who will not experience the outcome, more
positives means more false positives. The error rates are forced apart *by the
requirement that the number mean the same thing*.

## Where you can still make progress

The theorem does not end the engineering work. It relocates it. Three things
remain fully available, and you will use all three on page 7:

1. **Reduce the error rate.** The trade-offs are forced by imperfection. A better
   model shrinks the region in which they bind.
2. **Question the base rates.** The theorem takes base rates as given — but base
   rates are measured with your label. If the label encodes institutional
   behaviour rather than behaviour, the "difference in base rates" forcing the
   trade-off is an artefact of stage three. **This is the one that works on this
   dataset.**
3. **Change the decision.** The theorem constrains binary classification against
   a fixed threshold. Return a score with an interval, route uncertain cases to a
   human with time to consider them, offer an appeal, or intervene supportively
   rather than punitively.

> "Is this model fair?" is not a well-posed question. "Which fairness criterion
> does this decision require, on what account of the harm, at what measured cost
> to the others, agreed by whom?" is well posed, answerable and documentable.
> The theorem's real gift is that it makes the second question unavoidable.
